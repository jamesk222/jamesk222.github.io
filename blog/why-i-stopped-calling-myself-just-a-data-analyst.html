Why I Stopped Calling Myself "Just a Data Analyst"

20 Jul 2026·Career, BI

For most of the last decade, if you'd asked me what I do, I'd have said "data analyst" and left it there. Technically true. Also the least interesting way to describe the actual work.

A title like that tells a hiring manager what tools you touch. It says nothing about what changes because you touched them. And in BI specifically, that gap is the whole game — I've built dashboards that were technically flawless and completely ignored, and I've built rougher ones that reshaped a planning meeting in fifteen minutes. The difference was never the SQL.

What actually moved the needle

Looking back across fraud/churn models, forecasting pipelines and reporting automation work, the projects that mattered shared one trait: I started from the decision someone needed to make, not the dataset sitting in front of me. A 92%-precision churn model is a number. "We can now flag the 18% of at-risk accounts we were missing before renewal calls" is a decision.

The job was never "produce a dashboard." It was "change what happens on Monday morning."

Reframing the title

These days I describe the work as BI and marketing analytics with an AI-automation layer on top — because that's a more honest map of where the value actually sits: clean pipelines feeding trustworthy dashboards, narrated by AI-assisted reporting so non-technical stakeholders don't have to read forty charts to find the one number that matters.

It's a small wording change. But it's forced me to justify every project by its business outcome first, and its methodology second — which, unsurprisingly, is also what gets projects funded.
